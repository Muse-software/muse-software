# Experiment 4. We trust algorithms too much, then one mistake makes us trust them too little

## 1. Premise and audience job

A 2026 study on workplace AI use found that passive reliance was linked to lower self-reported confidence and ownership [28]. Two pre-LLM behavioural studies reveal a useful tension: people can prefer algorithmic advice to human advice [23], then abandon an algorithm faster than a human after seeing the same mistake [22]. The job for the reader: recognise their own trust pattern and explain where they switch from reliance to rejection.

## 2. Best format and channel adaptation

Instagram carousel as the primary format, paired with an X thread. LinkedIn frames it as a team decision rule: when should a person challenge, override, or keep an AI recommendation?

## 3. Truthful hook

"We trust algorithms too much. Then one mistake makes us trust them too little."

## 4. On-slide copy, 5 slides

**Slide 1**
We trust algorithms too much.
Then one mistake makes us trust them too little.

**Slide 2**
A 2026 study: 58% of participants said AI "did most of the thinking" on their task. Those participants reported less confidence in their own reasoning and less ownership of the work [28]. People who challenged or modified AI suggestions reported stronger confidence and authorship [28].

**Slide 3**
Algorithm appreciation, 2019: people followed advice more when they believed it came from an algorithm than when they believed it came from a person [23].

**Slide 4**
Algorithm aversion, 2015: after seeing the same mistake, people lost confidence in an algorithm faster than in a human, even when the algorithm had performed better [22].

**Slide 5**
Where do you switch from trusting AI to rejecting it, and is that line based on evidence or one bad answer?

## 6. Caption or opener

Our relationship with AI is not simple trust or distrust. Research suggests we can defer too easily, then overreact to one visible mistake. The useful question is how we decide when to challenge it.

## 7. Discussion question

Where do you switch from trusting AI to rejecting it, and what evidence moves that line?

## 8. Why it can earn engagement, by behaviour

- Comments: people naming the AI mistake that changed their behaviour, or the workflow where they still trust it
- Saves: a memorable behavioural paradox worth carrying into the next project
- Shares: sent to a teammate who either trusts the tool too quickly or rejects it after one failure
- Quote posts: practitioners adding their own rule for verification and override

## 9. Risks and how the published version avoids them

- The 2026 result concerns self-reported confidence and ownership, not measured intelligence. The copy says exactly that.
- The 2015 and 2019 studies are pre-LLM decision research. They explain behavioural concepts, not current chatbot performance, and are labelled by year.
- Algorithm appreciation and aversion can coexist because context, expertise, comparison target, and visible error all change behaviour. The post presents a tension, not a universal law.
- The 2026 source is 111 days old, past the 90 day target. Disclosed here rather than hidden.

## 10. Distribution plan

Personal account first: a founder posts in a reflective register. Muse amplifies within a few hours. Instagram uses the full carousel. LinkedIn adapts it into a team decision rule about when to challenge and override AI output.

## 11. Founder first-day interaction plan

- Before posting: reply usefully to 3 to 5 recent posts about AI trust, evaluation, or craft, with no link drops
- First 60 to 90 minutes: answer every substantive comment with a follow-up question about the person's evidence or workflow
- Quote-post targets: 1 to 2 researchers or practitioners who discuss algorithm trust, quoted to extend the question, never to claim endorsement
- Follow-up: group recurring replies into a second post on practical override rules, crediting useful contributors

## 12. Success hypothesis and metrics

Hypothesis: a behavioural paradox with a current AI example earns stories and disagreement rather than simple agreement. Primary metric: substantive replies. Secondary: quote posts and saves, with reach tracked but never celebrated on its own.
