This variance is not a statistical nuisance: the highest-performing strategy wins only 77% of head-to-head runs against its nearest competitor, meaning a single observed score can silently misrank systems. We introduce ReasonBench, a benchmark suite recording 30 independent trials across 10 reasoning strategies, 12 models, and 6 tasks, treating quality and cost as distributions rather than point estimates. We find that this variance is structured rather than random: a two-component taxonomy -- Global Noise, capturing cross-benchmark unevenness, and Run Noise, capturing within-benchmark stochasticity -- reveals that strategy architecture predicts stability profiles, while models and strategies shift orthogonal aspects of the distribution. A hierarchical decomposition attributes three-quarters of score variance to benchmark, system, and item structure, with a persistent residual that single-run evaluation silently absorbs. Finally, cost and quality decouple asymmetrically: cheap methods are structurally immune to joint cost-quality failure, while expensive methods remain exposed regardless of their accuracy. These findings establish instability as an inherent property of reasoning systems and motivate distribution-aware evaluation as standard practice.
