# Marketing Lead Hiring and Onboarding — Research Supplement

Outside-sourced hiring/screening/onboarding practice, to read alongside [[Marketing Lead Brief]], not instead of it. This does not restate that brief.

## TLDR

- The generic "marketing hire" checklist (channel breadth, campaign experience, analytics tools) is the wrong filter here. What matters more: someone who is already a practiced *personal* publisher, because distribution at Muse's stage runs through people, not a company account.
- Screen for a track record of actually shipping content publicly and sticking with a cadence past the boring middle — ask for the account, not the resume line. Portfolio > pitch.
- Native Arabic writing (not translation) is a hard filter, not a nice-to-have — mixed-market voice fails immediately if it reads translated.
- The single strongest predictor at this stage is comfort with ambiguity and zero-to-one work — this is closer to a first growth/marketing hire at a pre-PMF startup than a marketing-department hire at a normal SME (Lenny's Newsletter / Elena Verna, "Six rules of hiring for growth," 2021: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/six-rules-of-hiring-for-growth).
- Red-flag anyone who leans on vanity metrics, engagement-bait tactics, or can't describe a flop honestly — it directly conflicts with the "no fake numbers" stance already decided.
- First 30 days should not start with strategy decks — it starts with getting dormant accounts alive and a habit of publishing established, because a content account's biggest risk is silence, not imperfection.
- Treat the interview as evaluation of judgment and voice under real constraints (bilingual, part-time-possible, no budget yet), not a portfolio review of past employers' budgets.

## What actually matters here vs. a generic marketing hire

Generic marketing-hire criteria (paid acquisition experience, CRM/marketing-ops fluency, brand campaign management) assume an existing audience and budget to spend against. Muse has neither. The nearest real analogy is a startup's *first* growth/marketing hire before product-market fit is proven — and the standard advice there is explicit that this person should not be a narrow specialist or a "Head of Growth" title-chaser, but someone who can operate broadly and build the initial motion themselves (Elena Verna via Lenny's Newsletter: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/six-rules-of-hiring-for-growth). Verna's framing — growth model before growth hire, and match the hire's profile to the actual growth motion you're running — maps directly onto Muse: the motion here is content- and personal-brand-led, so the hire needs to be a practiced solo publisher/operator, not a manager of channels that don't exist yet.

A second, more anecdotal but concrete data point: First Round Review's account of Clay's early content hire (Mishti Sharma) describes a person who was skeptical of the product/topic but had "spiky" natural talent in writing and storytelling, given latitude to shape the role around her strengths rather than a pre-set job spec (First Round Review, "Firsthand: How I Bet On Clay (And It Bet On Me)": https://review.firstround.com/firsthand-how-i-bet-on-clay-and-it-bet-on-me-2/). *Opinion, not universal rule:* the lesson worth taking is that at this stage, the person's natural voice and what they're already good at matters more than fit against a job description written before anyone was in the room — which is consistent with how the Marketing Lead Brief is framed as an offer rather than a spec.

Traits that matter more here than in a typical marketing hire:
- **Demonstrated personal publishing habit** — an existing account, newsletter, or portfolio they built and sustained, in Arabic, English, or both. This is evidence, not a proxy.
- **Comfort operating with no brief and low resourcing** — the job starts from zero audience, zero case studies, likely part-time attention.
- **Native bilingual voice**, confirmed by writing samples in both languages rather than self-report.
- **Willingness to be public themselves** — the brief already asks the team to post under their own names; the lead has to model this first.

## Red flags to screen out

- Talks primarily in reach/impressions/follower-count terms, or their proof of past work is screenshots of metrics rather than the actual published content.
- Can't produce Arabic writing that reads as native when asked to write something on the spot (translated Arabic is detectable and undermines the voice standard already set).
- No visible personal presence online, despite claiming personal-brand expertise for others.
- Every project story is a success — no candid flop story, which the existing brief already asks for and which is also a general good-interview-signal per First Round Review's broader interviewing collections.
- Assumes big budget/team from day one, or pitches paid acquisition/growth-hacking tactics before content and audience exist — mismatched to a zero-audience starting point.
- Vague or evasive when asked directly about follower-buying, bot engagement, or "boosting" numbers — should be an immediate no given the "no fake numbers" stance already decided.

## Interview questions tailored to Muse's situation

1. "Show me an Arabic post or piece you wrote yourself, not translated — walk me through a word choice you debated." (Tests native voice claim directly, not self-report.)
2. "Pick one account, ours or someone else's, that grew almost entirely through the founder's or a team member's personal profile rather than a company page. What did they actually do week to week?" (Tests understanding of personal-brand-first distribution, not just company-brand thinking.)
3. "A post of ours is getting good engagement, but you suspect part of it is bot activity or a purchased boost from a client's other agency. What do you do?" (Direct stress-test of the no-fake-numbers stance.)
4. "You have five hours a week for this, not fifty. What's the one thing you'd protect no matter what?" (Tests realism about capacity vs. the brief's cadence-over-scale principle.)
5. "Tell me about a piece of content you made that flopped, and what you think actually caused it — not just 'the algorithm.'" (Tests honest self-diagnosis over excuse-making.)
6. "If client work takes over for two weeks and our own accounts go quiet, how do you decide what to sacrifice and what to protect?" (Tests fit with the shared-capacity reality already named in the brief.)

## First 30 days — grounded in Muse's actual gaps, not generic week-by-week advice

**Week 1 — make the dormant real.** The studio's actual blocker isn't strategy, it's that nothing exists publicly yet. Priority is closing that gap fast: get accounts activated with consistent bilingual bios/handles, publish something — even something small and imperfect — within the first days, and set up one place to capture raw material (voice notes, screenshots, half-written drafts) so content has a source before there's a system. Resist the pull to spend week 1 in planning docs; a studio with zero published content needs proof of motion before it needs a plan.

**Week 2 — pick the shape, not the whole strategy.** Now that something is live and got a real reaction (or non-reaction), decide the two or three channels to actually run, the cadence that survives a busy client week, and the first repeatable content format. This is where the open questions in the brief (which audience first, cadence, format) get resolved with real signal instead of guesswork, because week 1's posts already generated some evidence.

**Weeks 3–4 — build the buffer, not the campaign.** The realistic risk at Muse's stage isn't a bad post, it's the account going silent for a month once client work spikes — which the brief already flags as the actual failure mode. So the priority is building a small buffer of ready content (3–5 pieces) precisely so the first heavy client week doesn't kill momentum, and getting at least one other team member publishing under their own name, since that's the leverage point the brief identifies (personal accounts carry more reach locally than the company page).
