# Localisation Precedents

The evidence behind [[Product Philosophy]]. Real companies that took a proven model and won locally, plus the published thinking that supports the approach.

Written to be quotable. Anything not verified is marked, so nothing unchecked ends up in a document or in front of a client. Sources are at the bottom with live links.

## Saudi precedents

The four Abdullah named. All real, all local, all built on a model proven somewhere else first.

| Company | Model it took | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Careem | Ride hailing, the Uber model | Founded in Dubai in 2012, built for the region. Uber acquired it for USD 3.1 billion, completed January 2020 |
| Jahez | Food delivery | Saudi founded. Listed on Nomu, the Saudi parallel market, in 2022, priced at SAR 850 per share, implying a valuation around SAR 8.9 billion and raising about SAR 1.6 billion. Market capitalisation at debut was reported around USD 2.4 billion |
| HungerStation | Food delivery | Founded in Saudi Arabia in 2012. Majority acquired by Foodpanda in 2016, which took it into Delivery Hero. Delivery Hero bought the remaining 37 percent in July 2023 for about USD 297 million |
| Haraj | Online classifieds | Founded 2007 by Yousef Al Rasheedi. Started with cars, then property, then everything. Now one of the largest classifieds platforms in the region |

**Two corrections worth carrying.** Careem is Dubai founded rather than Saudi, which does not weaken the point since it is a regional adaptation of a global model and the largest exit the region has produced. And Haraj's closest global analogue is Craigslist rather than eBay: it is free form listings and direct contact between buyer and seller, not auctions. Getting that right matters if the example is used in front of anyone who knows the market.

**Unverified.** Jahez's exact listing date, HungerStation's revenue figures, and Haraj's daily user numbers all appeared in secondary sources with inconsistent values. Check against a primary source before printing any of them.

## International precedents

The pattern is not a Saudi trick. It is how most large consumer technology companies outside the United States were built.

**Grab and Gojek, Southeast Asia.** Both took the ride hailing model and beat Uber in their own markets, to the point that Uber sold its Southeast Asia business to Grab. They did not win on capital or on interface. They won on adaptation:

- Motorbikes rather than cars, because that is what moves through traffic in Jakarta and Hanoi
- Cash accepted long after Western platforms went card only, because most people were unbanked
- Then everything else on top of the same driver fleet: food, parcels, payments

That is the same shape as the three moves in [[Product Philosophy#Localisation is the product, not a translation layer]]. Local institutions, local context, local behaviour, and the localisation was product architecture rather than a settings screen.

**Meituan, China.** Wang Xing was called "The Cloner" for building Chinese versions of Friendster, Facebook, Twitter and Groupon. Meituan started as a Groupon clone, beat thousands of competitors, and became one of the most valuable companies in China. Kai-Fu Lee's argument in "AI Superpowers" is the interesting part: the copycat phase built a generation of operators with skills that did not exist in the market before, and those operators went on to build things nobody had copied.

That is the second order argument for this model and it applies directly to Muse. Even the attempts that do not become the golden egg leave a team that can ship.

## The published thinking

Useful because it means the approach is a documented strategy rather than a shortcut someone invented.

**Theodore Levitt, "Innovative Imitation", Harvard Business Review, September 1966.** The classic. Levitt argued that despite the near worship of innovation, most newness in business actually comes from imitation, and that companies should run a deliberate imitation strategy alongside original development. It won a McKinsey Award as one of the best HBR articles of that year. His sharpest finding: not one of the firms he studied had an imitation strategy, they all did it accidentally.

**Oded Shenkar, "Copycats: How Smart Companies Use Imitation to Gain a Strategic Edge", Harvard Business Review Press, 2010.** A whole book on the same argument. Innovators capture only a small share of the value they create, and disciplined imitators often capture more. Shenkar puts imitation at roughly 25 to 33 percent cheaper than innovation, because the imitator saves on research and on the market education the first mover already paid for.

Two things in Shenkar worth holding on to:

1. He is explicit that he is describing the legal forms of imitation only. That is exactly the line [[Open Questions#2. How close is too close]] has to draw for us
2. Imitation done well is a capability, not laziness. It has a method, and doing it badly is what produces a worse copy of somebody else's product

**Kai-Fu Lee, "AI Superpowers", 2018.** The copycat to innovator progression described above.

## The honest counterargument

Worth including in anything we publish, because leaving it out makes the document read as advocacy rather than thinking.

Peter Thiel's position in "Zero to One" is the well known opposing view: that copying what works takes the world from one to n, and that real value comes from doing something nobody has done, going from zero to one. It is a serious argument and it is not wrong about where the largest outcomes come from.

Our answer is not that Thiel is wrong. It is that the two are answering different questions. Zero to one is a strategy for a company that can survive years of uncertainty. We are three people funding ourselves with client work. Taking proven demand into a market that is genuinely underserved is the version of this that a company our size can actually execute, and the localisation depth in [[Product Philosophy]] is where our own originality goes.

## Market context

For anything that needs to establish that the Kingdom is a real market rather than a sentimental choice.

**Riyadh ranked 23rd among the top 100 emerging startup ecosystems** in the Global Startup Ecosystem Report 2025, published by Startup Genome with the Global Entrepreneurship Network. It moved up 60 places over three years. Note the wording: this is the emerging ecosystems ranking, not the overall global ranking, and stating it any other way is wrong.

**Government backed capital is real and named**, including the Public Investment Fund, Saudi Venture Capital Company, Jada, and Monsha'at as the small and medium enterprise authority. Relevant to [[Influence Strategy#3. Institutional access]] as much as to funding.

**Unverified.** Total venture funding figures for Saudi Arabia vary a lot between sources, from roughly USD 1.7 billion to over USD 4 billion depending on the year and what is being counted. Do not print a total without checking it against MAGNiTT or another primary tracker.
